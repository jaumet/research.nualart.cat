Australia · 1788–today

Who Drew These Lines?

Look at a map of Australia: most of the borders between its states are perfectly straight. They were not drawn by the people who lived there. They were drawn on paper, in London and in Sydney, across the Country of hundreds of nations who had lived on this land for more than 60,000 years. This is the story of who drew the lines, and why.

Start the story ↓

🔥 Chapter 1 · Before 1788

Country before the lines

For more than 60,000 years, the land we call Australia has been home to Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples. It is one of the oldest living cultures in the world.

  • 🗣️In 1788 there were hundreds of nations and more than 250 languages, with many more dialects. Many are still spoken today, and others are being learnt again.
  • 🌏Each nation looks after its Country: its land, waters, plants, animals and stories. Country is not only a place: people belong to it.
  • 💧The edges of Country follow rivers, hills, coasts and waterholes, and they are known through songs and stories. You will not find a ruler-straight line among them.

⛵ Chapter 2 · 1770–1788

A line through the desert

In 1770 the British sailor James Cook sailed along the east coast. On a small island in the north, he claimed the whole coast for his king and called it New South Wales.

  • ⚓On 26 January 1788 the First Fleet arrived at Sydney Cove with about 1,400 people, more than half of them convicts. Britain wanted a prison far away.
  • 📏The governor's orders said that New South Wales went from the east coast westward as far as 135° East, a line of longitude. No British person had ever walked there.
  • 📜The British acted as if the land was terra nullius, "nobody's land". There were no treaties, and nobody asked the people who lived there.

🇬🇧 Chapter 3 · 1825–1829

The whole continent claimed

Britain was afraid that France might take the west. So it moved its line, and then drew another colony.

  • ➡️In 1825 New South Wales moved its western line to 129° East. The same year, Van Diemen's Land (Tasmania) became a colony of its own.
  • 🦢In 1829 the Swan River Colony was founded at Perth. It claimed everything west of 129° East: today's Western Australia.
  • 🤔Now Britain claimed the whole continent. But on the ground, the colonists lived in a few small towns on the coast. Almost all the land was still lived on and looked after by its own peoples.

👆 The dashed line is 129° East. It is still the border of Western Australia today.

📜 Chapter 4 · 1836–1850

A colony with a promise

In 1836 a new colony was founded: South Australia. Its borders were lines on the map again: 132° and 141° East, and 26° South.

  • ✍️Its founding paper, the Letters Patent, said that the rights of the Aboriginal people to the land they lived on should not be affected. On the ground, the promise was broken: Kaurna land was sold to settlers.
  • 🐑Settlers moved inland with millions of sheep and cattle, far beyond the towns, and took the best land and water.

🛡️ Chapter 5 · 1790–1928

The frontier wars

This chapter tells sad things. If it upsets you, talk about it with an adult. These stories are remembered by many families today.

  • ✊Aboriginal people defended their Country wherever the settlers came. Leaders like Pemulwuy, Windradyne, Yagan and Jandamarra fought for years.
  • 🕯️Settlers, soldiers and police killed many thousands of people. Historians have recorded more than 400 massacres between 1788 and 1930.
  • 🦠New diseases, like smallpox, killed even more people. And many families lost their land, their water and their food.

📐 Chapter 6 · 1851–1863

The ruler lines

In just twelve years, the map got the borders it still has today.

  • ✨1851: Victoria separates from New South Wales, and gold is found. Hundreds of thousands of people arrive from all over the world.
  • 🌴1859: Queensland separates from New South Wales. In 1862 it grows west to 138° East.
  • 🧩1861–1863: South Australia grows west to 129° East and gets the land in the middle: the Northern Territory.

🎉 Chapter 7 · 1901

Six colonies become one country

On 1 January 1901 the six colonies joined together in a federation: the Commonwealth of Australia. The colonies became states, and they kept their borders.

  • 🗳️The colonists voted for the new Constitution. Very few Aboriginal people could vote, and the Constitution said that they should not be counted in the population.
  • 🚫One of the first laws, the "White Australia" policy, tried to stop people who were not white from coming to live in Australia.
💡

A new country, but the same old lines. Nobody thought of redrawing the borders around rivers or around peoples.

🏛️ Chapter 8 · 1911–1938

A capital and a territory

Sydney and Melbourne both wanted to be the capital. So a new city was built between them.

  • ✂️In 1911 New South Wales gave land to the Commonwealth for the Federal Capital Territory. The city of Canberra was designed by two architects from Chicago, Walter Burley Griffin and Marion Mahony Griffin. Parliament moved there in 1927.
  • 🔁The same year, South Australia handed the Northern Territory to the Commonwealth. From 1927 to 1931 it was even split in two along 20° South: North Australia and Central Australia.
  • 💔In these years governments took many Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander children away from their families. They are called the Stolen Generations.

🦘 Chapter 11 · Today

Six states and the territories

Today Australia has six states and, on the mainland, three territories: the Northern Territory, the Australian Capital Territory and the small Jervis Bay Territory .

  • 🏝️Australia also governs islands far away, like Norfolk Island and Christmas Island, and claims a big part of Antarctica.
  • 🌏Under the straight lines, the Country of every nation is still there. Today native title is recognised over large parts of Australia, and many places have their old names back.

📖 Key words

Little dictionary

🌏 Country
For Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples, the land, waters, sky and stories that a people belongs to and looks after.
🧑‍🤝‍🧑 Nation
A people with its own language, laws and Country. There were hundreds in 1788.
📏 Longitude and latitude
Imaginary lines that run from pole to pole (longitude, like 135° East) or around the Earth (latitude, like 26° South).
📜 Terra nullius
Latin for "nobody's land". The false idea that Australia belonged to nobody in 1788.
🏴 Colony
A land ruled by a foreign country, here Britain, and settled by people from there.
⛓️ Convict
A person sent far away as a punishment for a crime.
🛡️ Frontier wars
The fighting between Aboriginal people defending their Country and the settlers, soldiers and police who took it.
🤝 Federation
When several states join to make one country, but keep their own governments.
🏢 Territory
A part of Australia that is not a state. Its laws can be changed by the federal Parliament in Canberra.
💔 Stolen Generations
The Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander children taken away from their families by governments and churches.
⚖️ Native title
The recognition, in Australian law, that a people still has rights to its traditional land and waters.
✍️ Treaty
An agreement between peoples. Britain made none with the first peoples of Australia.

🍎 For teachers and families

How to use this page

About the maps

  • The colonial lines are drawn as they were written in commissions, letters patent and acts of Parliament. For most of the 19th century they were claims on paper: the colonists controlled only a small part of the land they show.
  • We do not draw the borders of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander nations. Maps of language areas exist (for example the one by AIATSIS), but they are simplified and protected by copyright, and communities do not always agree with them. The dots in chapter 1 show only a few nations, at today's cities.
  • All the maps are built from today's states, cut along the old lines. Small changes (the survey errors of the 141° East line, the Torres Strait islands annexed in 1872 and 1879) are simplified.
  • Percentages are measured on these simplified maps, with an equal-area projection.

Ideas for the classroom

  • Acknowledgement of Country: find out whose Country your school is on, and how the local community likes it to be acknowledged.
  • Your place: use "Your place through time" for your own town, then look for its name in the local language.
  • Two maps: compare the map of the states with a map of the rivers. Which one tells you more about where people lived?
  • Many voices: read about Pemulwuy, Vincent Lingiari or Eddie Mabo in sources written by Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander authors.
  • Links to the Australian Curriculum: History, years 4–6 (First Contacts, the Australian colonies, Federation) and years 9–10 (Rights and freedoms).
