The Indian subcontinent · 250 BCE–today

Who Drew These Lines?

The map of India, Pakistan, Bangladesh, Nepal, Bhutan, Sri Lanka and the Maldives has been drawn and redrawn many times. Empires grew and broke up, kingdoms came and went, a trading company took over, and new countries were born. This is the story of who drew the lines, and why.

Start the story ↓

🦁 Chapter 1 · About 250 BCE

Ashoka's messages in stone

More than 2,200 years ago, the Maurya Empire covered most of the subcontinent. Its most famous emperor was Ashoka. He ruled from Pataliputra, today Patna.

  • ⚔️Around 261 BCE, Ashoka's army conquered Kalinga (today Odisha). So many people died that Ashoka wrote he felt deep sorrow. He decided to rule by dhamma: kindness, honesty and respect for all.
  • 🪨He had his ideas carved on rocks and pillars all over the empire. They are called edicts. Their lion pillar at Sarnath is India's national emblem today.
  • 🗺️The edicts are like dots on a map: they show us how far the empire reached. In the far south, the edicts name neighbours who were not ruled by Ashoka: the Cholas, the Pandyas, the Keralaputras, the Satiyaputras and Tamraparni (Sri Lanka).

🕌 Chapter 2 · 1707

The Mughal Empire at its biggest

In 1707 the emperor Aurangzeb died. The Mughal Empire, started by Babur in 1526, was then at its biggest: from Kabul to Dhaka and from Kashmir to Tamil Nadu.

57%of the land of the subcontinent ruled directly by the Mughals (on this map)
~20provinces, called subahs, each with a governor
  • 🤝The Rajput kings kept their own kingdoms but served the emperor. In light green on the map are the rulers who paid tribute or were allies.
  • 🏔️Not everyone obeyed. The Marathas, after Shivaji, fought on from their hill forts. In the northeast, the Ahom kingdom of Assam had beaten a Mughal fleet at Saraighat in 1671.
  • ⛵On the coasts there were small European ports: Portuguese Goa, English Bombay, Madras and Calcutta, French Pondicherry and Dutch Cochin. For now they were only traders.

👑 Chapter 3 · About 1760

Many kingdoms, and some traders

Fifty years later the empire had broken into pieces. The emperor still sat on his throne in Delhi, but governors and generals had become kings.

  • 🚩The Marathas were the strongest power. From Pune, the Peshwa led a confederacy of families: Scindia, Holkar, Gaekwad and Bhonsle.
  • 🏰The Nizam ruled Hyderabad, the Nawabs ruled Bengal, Awadh and the Carnatic, and Mysore was about to be taken over by Hyder Ali.
  • 🐎From Afghanistan, Ahmad Shah Durrani attacked again and again. In January 1761 he beat the Marathas at the third battle of Panipat.
🤔

Look at Bengal. The little red patch near Calcutta is land of the British East India Company, a trading company from London. It has an army. Remember it!

🏴 Chapter 4 · 1757–1858

How a company took over

A company that came to buy cotton, silk, pepper and tea ended up ruling most of the subcontinent. How?

⚔️

Plassey, 1757

The Company beat the Nawab of Bengal, Siraj-ud-Daulah, with help from his own general, who had been promised the throne.

💰

Taxes, 1765

The Mughal emperor gave the Company the right to collect the taxes of Bengal, Bihar and Orissa. It paid for its army with Indian money.

🐯

Mysore, 1799

Tipu Sultan of Mysore fought four wars against the Company. He died defending his capital, Srirangapatna.

📜

Treaties and tricks

Many kings had to sign treaties, pay for Company troops and accept a British "resident" at court. Some kingdoms were simply taken when a king died without a son.

🔥

In 1857 soldiers and peasants rose up across the north, with leaders like Rani Lakshmibai of Jhansi. After the revolt was crushed, the British government took over from the Company in 1858.

🗺️ Chapter 5 · 1909

Red and yellow: the British Raj

By 1909 the whole map was under British rule, but in two different ways:

  • 🟥British India : provinces ruled directly by British officials, like the Bombay and Madras Presidencies, Bengal or Punjab.
  • 🟨Princely states : more than 560 kingdoms with their own Maharajas, Nawabs and Nizams. They ran their own affairs, but they had to obey the British in everything important.
37% of the land of the Indian Empire was in princely states (on this map, without Burma)

✂️ Chapter 6 · 1905–1911

The line that people rubbed out

In 1905 the Viceroy, Lord Curzon, cut Bengal in two. He said it was too big to govern. Many Indians thought the real reason was to divide them, because Bengal was the centre of the fight for self-rule.

  • 🧵People answered with Swadeshi: they stopped buying British cloth and goods and made their own.
  • 🎶Rabindranath Tagore wrote the song Amar Sonar Bangla ("My golden Bengal"). Today it is the national anthem of Bangladesh.
  • 🩹In 1911 the British gave in and put Bengal back together. At the same time they moved the capital from Calcutta to Delhi.
💡

A border drawn by rulers can be undone when many people work together.

🕊️ Chapter 8 · 1947

Freedom, and a painful partition

This chapter tells sad things. If it upsets you, talk about it with an adult. Many families still remember it.

  • 🎉On 15 August 1947 India became independent. The day before, Pakistan was created, in two parts: West Pakistan and East Pakistan.
  • 📏The new border was drawn by a British lawyer, Cyril Radcliffe, in about five weeks. He had never been to India before. It cut through Punjab and Bengal.
  • 🧳Around 15 million people had to leave their homes and cross the new border. There was terrible violence: historians estimate that between several hundred thousand and two million people died.
  • 🖊️The princely states (hatched on the map) had to join India or Pakistan. Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel and V. P. Menon persuaded almost all of them to join India. The last ones, like Hyderabad, joined in 1948–1949.

🗣️ Chapter 9 · 1956

States drawn by language

After 1947, many people asked for states where their language would be the language of schools, courts and offices.

  • 🕯️In 1952 Potti Sriramulu fasted until he died, asking for a state for Telugu speakers. Andhra State was created in 1953.
  • 📐On 1 November 1956, the States Reorganisation Act redrew the map. The colours show the main language of each state.
  • 🦓Striped states had two big languages. Look at Bombay State (Marathi and Gujarati) and Punjab (Punjabi and Hindi). What do you think happened next?

🇮🇳 Chapter 11 · Today

28 states and 8 union territories

Today India is a union of 28 states, with their own elected governments, and 8 union territories , governed more directly from New Delhi.

  • 🗣️The Constitution lists 22 languages, and hundreds more are spoken at home.
  • 🤝The neighbours, Pakistan, Bangladesh, Nepal, Bhutan, Sri Lanka and the Maldives, share much of the same long history you have just read.

📖 Key words

Little dictionary

👑 Empire
A big state that rules many lands and peoples, under one emperor.
🪨 Edict
An order or message from a ruler. Ashoka's edicts were carved in stone.
🏛️ Subah
A province of the Mughal Empire, ruled by a governor called a subahdar.
🤝 Tribute
Money or gifts that a weaker ruler pays to a stronger one, to be left in peace.
🚩 Confederacy
A group of rulers who act together, like the Maratha families.
🏴 Colonialism
When a foreign power rules a land to use its riches and its people.
🟨 Princely state
A kingdom inside British India, with its own ruler, under British control.
🟥 Presidency
One of the big provinces of British India: Bengal, Madras and Bombay.
✂️ Partition
The division of British India into India and Pakistan in 1947.
🖊️ Accession
When a princely state signed to join India or Pakistan.
🏢 Union territory
A part of India governed more directly by the central government in New Delhi.
🗣️ Linguistic state
A state whose borders were drawn so that most people share one language.

🍎 For teachers and families

How to use this page

About the maps

  • Before 1900, borders were not sharp lines. Power faded away from the cities and the roads; many forests, hills and deserts governed themselves. Our maps draw areas of control as simple shapes. Use them to compare, not to measure.
  • 250 BCE is drawn from the places of Ashoka's edicts and the usual reconstructions; historians debate how much the empire really controlled.
  • 1760 follows C. Colbeck's map India in the time of Clive, 1760 (1884), corrected with more recent atlases. 1909 follows the Imperial Gazetteer of India atlas: the yellow and red areas were read from a scan of the old map.
  • 1947, 1956 and today are built from today's states. The external boundaries of India follow the official position of the Government of India (Natural Earth, "India" point of view). Small changes, such as the hill districts that moved from Punjab to Himachal Pradesh in 1966, are drawn approximately.
  • Percentages are measured on these simplified maps, with an equal-area projection.

Ideas for the classroom

  • Edicts: read one of Ashoka's rock edicts in translation and discuss what "dhamma" means.
  • Your state: use "Your place through time" for your own city, then look for local monuments from each period.
  • Languages: list the languages spoken by the class at home. Would they fit in one state?
  • Many voices: look for freedom fighters from your region, especially women and Adivasi leaders, who are often missing from the big story.
  • Links to the NCERT history books: Our Pasts I–III (classes 6–8) and Themes in Indian History.
