Why in English? This atlas denounces colonialism, and English is one of the languages that colonialism imposed: it is still the official language of many African states. We publish it in English to reach more people, including the many Africans who mostly read in the language they were taught at school. That contradiction is part of the story this site tells.

An atlas of denunciation · 1870–today

Africa Carved Up

In 1884, in Berlin, fourteen powers met to divide up a continent where they had never set foot. There was not a single African in the room. Within thirty years, hundreds of kingdoms, empires, cities and peoples had been subjugated, plundered and divided by lines drawn with a ruler on incomplete maps. Those lines are still Africa's borders today.

      5 · Language diversity

      A continent of thousands of languages

      More than 2,000 languages are spoken in Africa, more than a quarter of the world's total according to Glottolog. They have no sharp borders: they share territory and overlap, and most people speak two or more.

      Colonial borders grouped hundreds of languages into a single state — more than 500 in Nigeria, according to Glottolog — and split many others apart.

        It was no one's empty land

        Nineteenth-century European maps painted the interior of Africa white. That blank was a convenient fiction: the doctrine of terra nullius made it possible to present conquest as the occupation of "empty" spaces. In reality, the continent was full of political systems: caliphates such as Sokoto, empires such as Asante or Lunda, kingdoms such as Buganda or Kongo, Swahili city-states, trading confederations and thousands of societies that organised themselves without a centralised state.

        On the 1880 map, the hatched areas are not empty: they are societies the dataset does not show, often without a centralised state, but also kingdoms and sultanates that the dataset leaves out . African borders were often flexible, negotiated, overlapping. Colonial borders, by contrast, were rigid, exclusive and drawn from outside.

        Straight lines over peoples

        Many borders were drawn along meridians, parallels and rivers on maps no one had checked on the ground. The result: peoples with a shared language and history — Maasai, Ewe, Bakongo, Somali, Hausa, Tuareg — were split between two, three or even five states, while within a single colony societies with no previous ties were lumped together.

        The violence did not end with the conquest. Genocides, forced labour, concentration camps, cultural plunder and wars of liberation marked the whole of the twentieth century. Many later conflicts have their roots in this design.

        An untouchable legacy

        In 1964 the new Organisation of African Unity decided to respect the inherited borders: redrawing them could have opened wars across the continent. This is the great paradox of decolonisation: the independent states had to preserve the division imposed by the colonisers. Only Eritrea (1993) and South Sudan (2011) have managed to change it.

        Remembering this process is not nostalgia for an idealised past. It is acknowledging an injustice that still has consequences, and that still awaits reparation, restitution and remembrance.

            Sources and method

            • Present-day borders: Natural Earth, Admin 0 – Countries 1:50m (public domain).
            • 1880 map: A. Ourednik, historical-basemaps (GPL-3.0), with corrections documented in the notes (Kingdom of Kongo, Transvaal, Lozi/Barotse, Aro Confederacy) and warnings where the geometry is doubtful.
            • 1914 map: our own reconstruction from present-day borders grouped by colonial unit, with the historical-basemaps pieces where the border was different (Kamerun, Togoland, Jubaland, Rudolf Province, the Spanish zones of Morocco). The original 1914 dataset had serious errors and has not been used directly.
            • Peoples: G. P. Murdock, Africa: Its Peoples and Their Culture History (1959), digitised by N. Nunn (CC BY-NC-SA 3.0). It shows approximate ethnic territories in the late 19th and early 20th centuries. Pejorative exonyms for cultural groups ("Galla", "Hottentot", "Bushmen") have been replaced with Oromo, Khoikhoi and San, and the Amazigh peoples, whom Murdock calls "Berbers" (from the Greek for "barbarian") and, in one case, by the Arabic name "Beraber", appear under their own name, Imazighen.
            • Sovereignty timeline: our own work on present-day borders, based on UNESCO's General History of Africa (vols. VII–VIII) and the literature cited. Conquest dates are approximate: effective control often came years after the formal claim.
            • Partition of peoples: our own calculation in an equal-area projection (Lambert azimuthal). A people counts as partitioned if at least 10% of its land lies in a second state, the same criterion as Michalopoulos and Papaioannou, "The Long-Run Effects of the Scramble for Africa", American Economic Review (2016).
            • Languages: H. Hammarström, R. Forkel, M. Haspelmath and S. Bank, Glottolog (Max Planck Institute for Evolutionary Anthropology), CC BY 4.0: location, family, vitality and last documentation of each language. Official languages: constitutions in force (see the notes).
            • Colonial violence: A. Hochschild, King Leopold's Ghost (1998); D. Olusoga and C. Erichsen, The Kaiser's Holocaust (2010); C. Elkins, Imperial Reckoning (2005); A. Adu Boahen (ed.), UNESCO General History of Africa VII (1985). Figures are estimates and are given as ranges where sources diverge.
